PART 1: CONCEPTS

14.3 Pediatric Dosing

All new, commercially manufactured drugs for use in the pediatric population are required, by law, to undergo studies prior to approval by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). This legislation, known as the Best Pharmaceuticals for Children Act of 2007, was an amendment to the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and was enacted to ensure the safety and efficacy of medications prescribed for children. Still, healthcare practitioners need to be mindful of the following axioms regarding pediatric dosing:

  • Many drugs have not been tested on children, so the “safe” dosage is not known.
  • Certain drugs should not be taken by children at any dosage.
  • Certain drugs should only be used to treat children after safer, alternative medications have been unsuccessful.

 

Pharm Fact

A prescriber often consults with a pharmacist to determine the appropriate pediatric dosage of an adult-strength medication.

To determine appropriate medications and dosing for pediatric patients, physicians often consult with pharmacists trained in pediatric pharmacotherapy. During these consultations, pharmacists commonly use reference materials such as Trissel’s Stability of Compounded Formulations; Handbook on Injectable Drugs; LexiComp; MicroMedex; or Pediatric Injectable Drugs, commonly known as The Teddy Bear Book.

 

 

Adult-Strength Formulations

Many commercially available medications are only available in adult-strength formulations. Consequently, healthcare practitioners often need to prescribe adult-strength formulations and modify the dosage to appropriately treat smaller, pediatric patients. Examples of commercially available, adult-strength formulations commonly used to prepare CSPs for pediatric patients include clindamycin, dexamethasone, digoxin, furosemide, gentamicin, and vancomycin.

 

 

Modifying an Adult-Strength Formulation

The practice of modifying adult-strength formulations to treat children is challenging. Due to the highly concentrated nature of most adult-strength formulations, the volume necessary to administer a “child-sized” dose to a neonatal or a pediatric patient may be too small to measure accurately. This concept is illustrated as follows.

 

 

Adult-Strength Formulation Example

A prescriber has ordered a 2 mg dose of preservative-free (PF) gentamicin for a neonatal patient weighing 1 kg. The label of the adult-strength formulation of gentamicin states that the stock medication has a concentration of 40 mg/mL (see Figure 14.1).

 

Figure 14.1 Adult-Strength Formulation of Gentamicin: Gentamicin is an injectable antibiotic that is used to treat an infection. © Paradigm Education Solutions

 

In this scenario, sterile compounding personnel perform the following calculation   to determine the pediatric dose:

 

\(\frac{40\text{ mg} }{1\text{ mL} }=\frac{2\text{ mg} }{x\text{ mL} }\)

1 × 2 = 2

2/40 = 0.05

x = 0.05 mL

 

The resulting volume, 0.05 mL, is too small for personnel to draw up accurately in a syringe.

 

 

Pediatric-Strength Formulations

Some manufacturers have reformulated certain adult-strength medications into diluted concentrations to accommodate the unique physiologic needs of pediatric patients. Examples of pediatric-strength formulations commonly used in the preparation of CSPs for pediatric patients include gentamicin, lorazepam, and tobramycin. When a pediatric-strength formulation is not available, a stock solution may be needed. The Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) recommends that standard concentrations be used to help avoid dosing errors (see Table 14.1).

 

In addition to ISMP, other organizations have provided recommendations for pediatric standard concentrations. For example, the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP) has published Standardize 4 Safety for both parenteral and oral preparations, and the Michigan Pediatric Safety Collaboration has published Standardize Compounded Oral Liquids for oral preparations. A detailed discussion of the ISMP and ASHP initiatives, which pertain to sterile compounding, are discussed later in this chapter.

digoxin 100 mcg/mL

Using a Pediatric-Strength Formulation

There is a limited selection of pediatric-strength formulations. However, the use of these medications makes the preparation of CSPs easier and more accurate, as shown in the following example.

 

 

Pediatric-Strength Formulation Example

A prescriber has ordered a 2 mg dose of PF gentamicin for a neonatal patient weighing 1 kg. The label of the pediatric-strength formulation of gentamicin states that the stock medication has a concentration of 20 mg/2 mL, or 10 mg/mL (see Figure 14.2).

 

Pharm Fact

Sterile compounding personnel should be aware that some drug manufacturers always capitalize the name of the drug on the label, regardless of whether the medication is a generic name or a brand name. For that reason, personnel should be sure to memorize both the generic and brand names of drugs.

Figure 14.2 Pediatric-Strength Formulation of Gentamicin: Gentamicin 20 mg/2 mL injection is an example of a medication that comes in a pediatric-strength formulation. © Paradigm Education Solutions

 

In this scenario, sterile compounding personnel perform the following calculation to determine the pediatric dose:

 

\(\frac{10\text{ mg} }{1\text{ mL} }=\frac{2\text{ mg} }{x\text{ mL} }\)

1 × 2 = 2

2/10 = 0.2 mL

x = 0.2 mL

 

The resulting volume, 0.2 mL, can be easily drawn up in a 1 mL syringe by personnel.

 

 

Pediatric Dosing and Medication Errors

Practice Tip

To download and review TJC’s sentinel event on the prevention of pediatric medication errors, sterile compounding personnel should visit the following location: https://SterileComp2.ParadigmEducation.com/TJCSentinelEvent.

Modifying adult-strength formulations for the treatment of children has resulted in an increase in medication errors in pediatric patients. The majority of these medication errors are related to an incorrect dose or quantity of a drug. In response to the increased number of errors, The Joint Commission (TJC) issued a sentinel event alert in 2008. This alert warned healthcare facilities of the dangers of modifying adult- strength formulations for pediatric use:

 

Research shows that the potential for adverse drug events within the pediatric inpatient population is about three times as high as among hospitalized adults…. Children are more prone to medication errors and resulting harm because most medications used in the care of children are formulated and packaged primarily for adults. Therefore, medications often must be prepared in different volumes or concentrations within the health care setting before being administered to children. The need to alter the original medication dosage requires a series of pediatric-specific calculations and tasks, each significantly increasing the possibility of error.

 

The increased risk associated with the modification of adult-strength formulations for children has led to the recommendation of pediatric standard concentrations by the ISMP and other organizations.
