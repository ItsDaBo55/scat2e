PART 1: CONCEPTS

7.3 Dosage Calculations

Best Practice

In some facilities, a sterile compounding technician assembles the CSP using information gleaned only from the CSP label. However, it is considered best practice for both a technician and a pharmacist to review both the medication order and the CSP label to ensure accuracy. Sterile compounding personnel should consult their facility’s Policy and Procedures (P&P) manual to determine this process.

The calculations that sterile compounding personnel must perform are most often based on information found on the CSP label and the label on the stock ingredient (also called the stock medication or stock drug) container. This container may be either a vial or an ampule and may be supplied in either a powdered or liquid form.

 

Personnel begin the calculation process by determining the concentration of the ingredient. This information can be found on the ingredient label of the stock ingredient’s container. They then use the ingredient’s concentration and the ordered dose from the medication order or CSP label to perform a calculation or series of calculations using one of the calculation methods provided in this chapter. These calculations determine the volume of each ingredient needed to prepare the CSP.

 

To help with these calculation tasks, Figure 7.1 identifies the components of a label on a stock ingredient, including the printed information that identifies the concentration or strength of the ingredient, which is necessary for the preparation of a CSP. Locating and understanding label information are important initial steps in ensuring accuracy in pharmacy calculations and, consequently, in sterile compounding.

Pharm Fact

Sterile compounding personnel should be aware that some drug manufacturers always capitalize the name of the drug on the stock ingredient label, regardless of whether the ingredient has a generic name or a brand name. For that reason, personnel should be sure to memorize both the generic and brand names of drugs.

Special Considerations for Calculations

When performing calculations, sterile compounding personnel must be aware of some special considerations. These considerations include the situations that dictate when to use the volume and strength of the base solution during calculations and the scenarios that must take into account overfill volume of the base solution.

 

 

Volume and Strength of the Base Solution

One consideration is that the volume (in milliliters [mL] or liters [L]) and strength (in percent [%]) of the base solution are not to be included in any equation in which personnel are asked to determine the amount or volume of drug that is to be drawn up for a desired dose. Sterile compounding personnel may be asked to determine how long an intravenous (IV) bag will last or the number of IV bags needed (such as in the calculation of IV flow rates). For these scenarios, they must use the volume of the base solution in the calculations; however, the strength of the base solution is not needed. Generally, the only time sterile compounding personnel will need to include the strength of the base solution in a calculation is during the preparation of parenteral nutrition (PN) or during supply shortages of base solutions. For specific information on PN preparation, refer to Chapter 15 of this textbook.

 

 

Overfill Volume of the Base Solution

Overfill is another special consideration when performing calculations for sterile compounding. Overfill is the amount of extra fluid that a manufacturer adds to a commercially prepared IV solution to account for fluid that remains inaccessible in a source container, tubing, or needle-and-syringe unit. Consequently, the volume of the solution in the container is greater than the volume indicated on the label. The amount of overfill varies among manufacturers but is typically 5%–10% of the total volume specified on the container’s label. For example, a 100 mL intravenous piggyback (IVPB) solution typically contains 5–10 mL of overfill.

 

In some sterile compounding situations, the amount of overfill does not need to be considered when performing pharmacy calculations. Sterile compounding situations that may fall in this category include those situations that meet one of the following criteria:

 

  • The CSP is a simple admixture comprised of a single additive, and the additive has a relatively small volume.
  • The CSP is intended to be administered over a short period (e.g., 60 minutes).
  • The CSP is comprised of a medication that is to be infused at a rate determined by the effect of the medication on the patient and not on the volume or dose administered (e.g., diltiazem titrated to effect).

 

Other compounding situations require that the overfill volume either be removed from the LVP or SVP solution prior to compounding or that the overfill volume be included in the calculation. Sterile compounding situations that may necessitate one of these actions include compounding scenarios for pediatric and neonatal CSPs, narcotic preparations, chemotherapy CSPs, and PN solutions. These types of CSPs are discussed in later chapters of this textbook.

 

Sterile compounding facilities should have written protocols for handling overfill. Personnel should check their facility’s Policy and Procedures (P&P) manual for specific information related to overfill in various compounding scenarios.
