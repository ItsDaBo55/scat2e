Review and Assessment

Chapter Summary

  • Pediatrics is a medical specialty focusing on the care and treatment of children.
  • Pediatric medicine is based on several factors: a patient’s birth or gestational age, physiology (kidney, liver, cardiovascular, and respiratory functions), body size, and weight.
  • Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi is referred to as the “Father of Pediatrics” for treating childhood diseases such as smallpox and measles and for having written the book The Diseases of Children.
  • In the second century, Greek physician Soranus wrote a manuscript detailing several childhood diseases.
  • The first printed book that recognized common diseases affecting children was Bagallarder’s Little Book on Disease in Children, published in Italy in 1472.
  • The Boke of Chyldren, written by English physician Thomas Phaire, was the first English-language book to discuss childhood conditions.
  • In the 17th century, Dr. Thomas Sydenham wrote about treatments for children who were afflicted with scarlet fever, measles, epilepsy, and scurvy. Sydenham’s work dramatically improved medical understanding of these diseases.
  • In 1798, Dr. Edward Jenner’s published pediatric research led to further experiments that resulted in the development of a smallpox vaccine, which was heralded as one of the wonder drugs of the 19th century.
  • US physicians who made notable contributions to the advancement of pediatric medicine in the 19th century include Dr. Abraham Jacobi, Dr. John Forsyth Meigs, Dr. Luther Holt, and Dr. William M. Marriott.
  • New York physician Dr. Abraham Jacobi is considered the “Father of American Pediatric Medicine.”
  • Pediatricians must have a minimum of 11 years of higher education: 4 years of college, 4 years of medical school, 1 year of a pediatrics internship, and 2 years of a pediatrics residency.
  • Pediatricians who specialize in the treatment of neonates (or infants under one month of age) are called neonatologists.
  • In addition to pediatric units, some hospitals have neonatal and pediatric intensive care units for children who are critically ill.
  • Adjusting adult medication doses for pediatric patients is still a common pharmacy practice; however, some drug companies have channeled significant resources toward the research and development of medications formulated specifically for children.
  • The Pediatric Pharmacy Association (PPA) promotes pediatric pharmacy research as well as advocacy for pediatric pharmacy issues, such as decreasing pediatric medication errors.
  • Specially trained pharmacists work closely with prescribers to recommend appropriate drug therapy, monitor clinical outcomes, and perform drug-utilization review for pediatric patients. These pharmacists must be familiar with pediatric physiology, as well as drug disposition, dosage modification procedures, and pharmacotherapeutic treatment of neonatal and pediatric patients.
  • When determining drug dosages for children, prescribers must consider several factors, including a wide-ranging age group, age-specific variations in pharmacokinetics, and a variable body weight and BSA.
  • The Best Pharmaceuticals for Children Act of 2007—an amendment to the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act—dictates that new commercially manufactured drugs for use in the pediatric population must undergo studies prior to approval by the FDA.
  • Most commercially available medications are only available in adult-strength formulations.
  • The practice of modifying adult-strength formulations to treat children has resulted in an increase in medication errors in the pediatric patient population, therefore prompting the use of standard concentrations.
  • Pediatric dilutions are based on pediatric standard concentrations, or established concentrations that pharmacists use to determine drug-to-volume ratios. Pediatric standard concentrations are recommended by the ISMP and the ASHP.
  • Pediatric CSPs are sterile solutions that typically have a volume of 500 mL or less. Common pediatric CSPs include IVP antibiotics, LVP solutions, and TPN solutions.
  • Preservative-free ingredients are typically used in the preparation of CSPs for neonates and preterm infants. Many facilities extend this rule for pediatric patients age five or younger.
  • Pediatric CSPs are typically administered via a continuous IV infusion or as intermittent doses using the IVP route.
  • Two risks associated with the administration of pediatric CSPs include medication overdose and hypervolemia.
  • Programmable infusion pumps are helping to eliminate medication errors related to the administration of CSPs.
  • Anteroom preparatory procedures include verifying the labels for the pediatric standard concentration and the patient-specific CSP, performing calculations, gathering and staging supplies, performing hand hygiene, and garbing.
  • Buffer room preparatory procedures include cleaning and disinfecting the PEC and using aseptic technique to prepare the pediatric standard concentration within the DCA of the PEC.
  • The procedural lab requires the preparation of a pediatric dilution in an EEC based on a standard concentration; the patient-specific dose is then withdrawn from the EEC into a syringe to which a sterile syringe cap and CSP label are applied.
