The Art of Early Pharmacy Practices

The origins of pharmacy can be traced back to primitive civilizations where healers in these communities used local flowers, herbs, and other natural substances to artfully compound remedies for the treatment of illness and disease. These early inhabitants believed that sickness was divine punishment from spirits or gods. Therefore, the administration of the compounded natural remedies was often accompanied by chants, prayers, and other rituals to rid the victim’s body of the evil curse.

 

The Alchemist by David Teniers the Younger (1582–1649); its date is unknown, but it is currently displayed at the Museo del Prado in Madrid, Spain. Source: US National Library of Medicine

 

Cultures from every continent have long demonstrated an understanding of the healing properties of natural substances, as supported by the numerous, detailed prescriptions uncovered in archaeological digs. These remedies were for common ailments that beset the region’s inhabitants, including sleep disorders, skin conditions, heart ailments, common cold symptoms, and fevers. Much of the local flora that was harvested for the compounding of these remedies is still the source of several medications that healthcare practitioners prescribe today: opium poppy, aloe, foxglove, echinacea, ginkgo, feverfew, and ginger, to name a few.

 

Egyptian papyrus depicting a scene of royalty; early advanced medical understanding was typically reserved for royalty and the wealthy but often drew from the common knowledge of trial and error and inherited remedies. © leoks/Shutterstock.com

 

Evidence of medicinal compounding can also be found in the histories of several religions. Ancient Islamic texts describe the healing properties of plants, such as hindiba and lemon balm, to promote wellness. The excavation of numerous Egyptian tombs provides evidence that religious ceremonies, such as burials, used compounded ointments, salves, and perfumes made from tree resins, cassia, and camphor. These natural substances were used to prepare the bodies for the afterlife. The Christian Nativity story is another example of the link between religion and the origin of pharmacy. This story tells of a wise man who gifts the healing resin myrrh to the newborn baby Jesus. Myrrh is still used today as a homeopathic treatment for inflammatory conditions of the mouth and gums and as a topical agent for the healing of hemorrhoids, bedsores, and wounds.

 

Freshly picked lavender, a popular aromatic ingredient in ancient remedies; essential oil of lavender has been found to have antiseptic and anti-inflammatory properties. © matka_Wariatka/Shutterstock.com

 

Cultural expansion across the globe led to the exchange and refinement of natural remedies by ancient healers. Their carefully documented recipes and practices laid the foundation for more formalized medicinal compendiums and the establishment of the healing art known today as pharmacy.

 

 

Pharmacy Incorporates Science

Two early Greek practitioners were instrumental in the development of pharmacy practices: Hippocrates (ca. 460 bce – ca. 370 BCE) and his successor, Galen (129 CE – 216 CE). Hippocrates is often referred to as “The Father of Medicine” and is credited with encouraging a scientific approach to medicine and pharmacy, helping to debunk the early myths that illness was caused by evil spirits. Hippocrates believed that illness was caused by a patient’s lifestyle (diet, exercise, sleep patterns) and surrounding environment (quality of air, water, and soil in contact with the individual). His observations hold true thousands of years later, for these factors have been proven to affect the health and well-being of individuals.

 

A 1677 drawing of Galen and Hippocrates by Justus Cortnumm. Source: US National Library of Medicine

 

Hippocrates also developed the concept of the body’s four humors: blood, phlegm, black bile, and yellow bile. Each humor was associated with a particular organ: blood with the heart; phlegm, the lungs; black bile, the spleen; and yellow bile, the gallbladder. When the humors were harmonious and in balance, an individual maintained a healthful state, or eucrasia (meaning “good mixture”). If one humor became excessive and upset the body’s equilibrium, dyscrasia (meaning “bad mixture”) occurred, and the individual’s health was compromised.

 

Galen is commonly referred to as “The Father of Pharmacy” for his efforts to systematically classify the remedies used to treat illness and disease. Like Hippocrates, Galen believed in the importance of maintaining equilibrium among the four humors. However, he argued that these humors must be kept in balance within individual organs rather than within the body as a whole. Although the theory of the four humors was later disproved, the research of Galen and Hippocrates laid the foundation for the exploration of the medical and pharmaceutical sciences.

 

Galen’s position as a surgeon for Roman gladiators contributed to his knowledge of human anatomy and physiology. His observations of the structures and functions of the heart’s valves, arteries, and veins influenced medical knowledge for more than 1,000 years. Although some of Galen’s theories regarding the roles of these vessels in blood circulation proved later to be inaccurate, he is credited for establishing the differences between arterial and venous blood.

 

Works of historic Greek literature, such as Homer’s The Iliad and The Odyssey, also tell the tales of some of the women who were instrumental in the development of ancient pharmacy. For example, Agamede, Princess of Elis, was a well-known healer who used herbal compounds and medicinal tinctures to treat soldiers wounded in the Trojan War.

 

 

Establishment of the Apothecary Model

During the Middle Ages, the pharmacy profession in Europe adopted the apothecary model. Apothecary shops sprang up in villages across the continent. Residents of these villages received both diagnosis and treatment from the specialists who worked in these shops. These practitioners would grind and mix flowers, herbs, tree resins, and other natural ingredients using a mortar and pestle. Because of its use in creating medicinal preparations, the mortar and pestle would later serve as a symbol of the pharmacy profession.

 

Stone mortar and pestle; an ancient apothecary symbol. © yienkeat/Shutterstock.com

 

 

Early Drug Compounding

Like their European counterparts, most colonial apothecaries in the United States compounded herbal medicine using the recipes of pharmacy texts they brought over from England. As the colonies grew in the 18th century, they attracted a broader range of immigrants, including physicians who prescribed, prepared, and dispensed drugs imported from England. The treatment of wounded soldiers during the American Revolution forced these physicians to prepare their own crude drugs from chemicals and natural substances.

 

Vintage apothecary bottles and scale from the turn of the twentieth century. © Bruno Monico/Shutterstock.com

 

In 1820, the first official listing of drugs in the United States, the Pharmacopoeia of the United States of America, was published by the Massachusetts Medical Society, with approval from a national convention of physicians. This publication provided uniformity in formulations. As the century wore on, healthcare practitioners who were newly trained by medical schools set up practices and prepared their own medications. They slowly moved away from compounding drugs and focused solely on the diagnosis and treatment of their patients. In 1852, the American Pharmaceutical Association (now known as the American Pharmacists Association or APhA) was formed, releasing physicians from the task of compounding medicines and allowing pharmacists the opportunity to hone their skills and increase their professional stature. However, the boundaries between the professions of physician and pharmacist were not clearly established until after the US Civil War (1861–1865).

 

Old glass storage bottles of the type commonly found on the shelves of apothecary shops; sealed with corks and often wax, bottles like these were not effective preservation for rapidly degrading or sensitive medical substances. iStock.com/BMPix

 

Vintage syringe and sterilizing box. Sterilizing boxes were used by medics during World War I and World War II. iStock.com/Maica

 

 

Relationship Between Illness and Infection

As physicians and pharmacists established their own practice standards in the 19th century, healthcare practitioners in Europe made significant advances in understanding sanitary conditions and infection-control measures. The work of Hungarian obstetrician Ignaz Semmelweis in 1847 influenced hospital awareness of infection control. At Vienna General Hospital in Austria, Semmelweis recognized the role that handwashing played in reducing infection and mortality among his maternity patients.

 

English nurse Florence Nightingale observed the importance of cleanliness and sanitary conditions firsthand as she provided treatment for wounded soldiers during the Crimean War. Her observations of the importance of implementing infection-control measures became a catalyst for healthcare reform in England.

 

In 1867, British surgeon Joseph Lister used the sterile practices set forth by Semmelweis and Nightingale for his research on the germ theory of disease. Lister’s experiments with sterilization techniques proved that cleansing hands, donning gloves, and using germicides such as carbolic acid on surgical instruments and sutures greatly reduced infection.

 

Finally, in the 1870s, the research of French scientist Louis Pasteur confirmed to physicians the importance of handwashing and proper sanitization in preventing the transmission of disease.

 

 

Rise of Pharmaceutical Manufacturing

By the beginning of the 20th century, pharmaceutical manufacturing began to take hold. Although many pharmacists continued to rely on plants as ingredients in their compounded preparations, they now used their understanding of chemistry, botany, and herbology to discover and synthesize medications to treat a wide variety of conditions. Compounding practices evolved from the creative, but slower, trial-and-error process of mixing tinctures, balms, and liniments to the precise sterile compounding procedures performed in today’s sleek, modern pharmacy facilities. The practice known as sterile compounding evolved from the advancements made in both compounding procedures and infection-control measures.

 

A 1913 image of the interior of a pharmaceutical factory in Höchst, a district in Frankfurt, Germany. With a new understanding of germ transmission and infection control, factory personnel are working behind a type of splash guard that has been erected to avoid contaminants from nose and mouth secretions. Image courtesy of Period Paper

 

Although pharmacy has gained a scientific and technical framework over the past 150 years, its association as an art remains. In fact, the first definition of the term pharmacy in Merriam-Webster’s Collegiate Dictionary is “the art, practice, or profession of preparing, preserving, compounding, and dispensing medical drugs.”

 

 

Unit 1 Overview: Seven Exploratory Labs

The chapters in Unit 1 are arranged using a stepped approach, with each chapter building on the preceding one. Chapter 1 provides an overview of sterile compounding facilities and the education, training, and roles and responsibilities of personnel who prepare compounded sterile preparations (CSPs). This chapter also introduces the concept of aseptic technique in the preparation of these CSPs. Chapter 2 addresses medication safety, including the reasons for medication errors, risk-reduction strategies for the compounding and administration of medications, detection and reporting of medication errors, and medication safety resources for pharmacy personnel. Chapter 3 focuses on the legal and ethical issues faced by sterile compounding personnel and the importance of having a quality assurance program. Chapter 4 builds on that foundational information, expanding into the sterile compounding environment and the most important and basic tool for sterile compounding: the primary engineering control, which is typically a hood. Chapter 5 introduces the additional supplies and equipment used by personnel while working in the anteroom and buffer room, more commonly referred to as the cleanroom suite. Finally, Chapters 6 and 7 explain the initial steps in the sterile compounding process itself: interpreting medication orders, reading pharmacy labels, and performing pharmacy calculations.

 

Each chapter in Unit 1 contains three parts: Concepts, Training, and Assessment. The Training section has an “Exploratory Lab,” or a hands-on lab activity focused on the roles, responsibilities, or work environment of sterile compounding personnel. These chapters provide a firm foundation of the basics of sterile compounding procedures before performing the “Procedural Labs” in Unit 2.
