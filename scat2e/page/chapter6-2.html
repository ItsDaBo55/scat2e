PART 1: CONCEPTS

This chapter provides a general discussion of medical terminology and addresses the contents and processing of a medication order. To supplement this discussion, Appendix A of this textbook provides a series of tables outlining the most frequently used medical terms; common root words, prefixes, and suffixes; and common medical and pharmacy abbreviations, acronyms, and symbols. These tables can also be accessed on the Cirrus learning management system that accompanies this textbook.

 

 

6.2 Medical Terminology

Name Exchange

Individuals who have been trained, demonstrated competency, and–where applicable–certified in sterile compounding and aseptic technique are the only personnel permitted to compound sterile preparations. These individuals may be referred to collectively as sterile compounding personnel or IV personnel. They may also be referred to by their positions in a sterile compounding facility: sterile compounding pharmacists or IV pharmacists, or sterile compounding technicians or IV technicians.

The origin of medical terminology, also called medical terms, is as old as medicine itself. Many of the thousands of medical terms commonly used today in medicine and pharmacy can be traced back to the 4th-century medical treatments developed by Hippocrates and others who shaped early medical science. As a result, approximately 75% of medical terminology has its roots in the Greek language. Much of the remainder can be traced to Latin, the language of medical texts until the beginning of the 18th century.

 

Over the centuries, medical terminology has developed in parallel with advancements in the diagnosis and treatment of illness, injury, and disease. (Refer to Table 1 in Appendix A for a list of common medical terms.) Today, there are more than 100,000 entries in medical dictionaries and 35,000 abbreviations, acronyms, and symbols used in pharmacy and medicine. For that reason, sterile compounding personnel need to have a good understanding of the medical terminology used by prescribers.

 

 

Medical Root Words, Prefixes, and Suffixes

Most medical terminology is based on a combination of medical morphemes. A morpheme is the smallest meaningful unit of a spoken language. Examples of morphemes include root words, prefixes, and suffixes.

 

 

Root Words

A word’s meaning is derived from its root word (sometimes called the word root). All medical terms have at least one root word, and these roots typically refer to regions or organs of the body or to pathological processes or conditions. (Refer to Table 2 in Appendix A for a list of common medical root words.) To make pronunciation of a root word easier and to assist with a root word’s connection to a suffix, a combining vowel is sometimes added to the end of a root word. The combining vowel most commonly added in medical terminology is o; however, the vowels i and e may also be used. When a root word contains this combining vowel, the word is said to be in its combined form. For example, the root word gastr, meaning “stomach,” is most often seen in its combined form as gastr/o or gastro. This combined form of the root word facilitates the English-language spelling and pronunciation of the medical term when the suffix –pathy is added. The medical term in its combined form is gastropathy, which means “disease of the stomach.”

Study Idea

To help in the memorization of commonly used medical terminology, abbreviations, and symbols, sterile compounding students may want to create flash cards to assess their knowledge.

 

 

Prefixes

A prefix is a word element attached to the beginning of a root word. In medical terminology, a prefix often indicates time, location, direction, or number. Some common prefixes include pre– meaning “before,” post– meaning “after,” hypo– meaning “under” or “below,” and hyper– meaning “above” or “excessive.” Changing the prefix can significantly impact the meaning of a medical term. For example, the term hypoglycemia contains the prefix hypo– and refers to an abnormal decrease in blood glucose. In contrast, the term hyperglycemia contains the prefix hyper– and refers to an abnormal increase in blood glucose. (To view other common medical prefixes, see Table 3 of Appendix A.)

 

 

Suffixes

A suffix is a word element attached to the end of a root word. The addition of a suffix further defines the meaning of a root word. Some common suffixes include –ectomy meaning “surgical removal,” –tomy meaning “surgical incision,” and –pathy meaning “disease.” For example, the term appendectomy contains the suffix –0ectomy and refers to the surgical removal of the appendix. (For a list of additional common medical suffixes, see Table 4 of Appendix A.)

 

 

Medical and Pharmacy Abbreviations, Acronyms, and Symbols

Safety Alert

Sterile compounding personnel should exercise caution when using and interpreting abbreviations, acronyms, and symbols. Several abbreviated forms are ambiguous or nonstandard. The convenience of using abbreviations must never outweigh the potential risk for medication error that may result from their misinterpretation.

As mentioned earlier, there are thousands of abbreviations, acronyms, and symbols used in medicine and pharmacy. Collectively, these abbreviations, acronyms, and symbols are referred to as medical abbreviations or, simply, abbreviations. Most abbreviations are developed to shorten the length of words and word combinations related to health, medicine, laboratory procedures or tests, and pharmacy. Healthcare professionals communicate with each other quickly, easily, and effectively by using these abbreviated forms. For example, it is faster and more convenient for a physician or other prescriber to write or type the abbreviation TPN on a medication order than to record the words total parenteral nutrition.

 

To provide safe and effective health care, all sterile compounding personnel must have a thorough understanding of medical and pharmacy abbreviations, acronyms, and symbols. Although learning these common abbreviations can be a challenging task for pharmacy personnel, the importance of knowing this specialized “language” cannot be overstated.

 

 

Common Medical Abbreviations and Acronyms

Abbreviations refer to many components of health care. For example, abbreviations may indicate specific patient care facilities, units, or departments (e.g., the abbreviation ICU meaning “intensive care unit”), or the titles of individual team members (e.g., the abbreviation RN meaning “registered nurse”). Abbreviations also refer to specific medical diagnoses and conditions, procedures, treatments, patient directives, patient activities, equipment/supplies, and tests. Some examples of these abbreviations include CVA meaning “cerebrovascular accident,” CBC meaning “complete blood count,” and NC meaning “nasal cannula.” In fact, most of the hundreds of laboratory and diagnostic procedures and tests performed in a medical setting have corresponding abbreviations that are more frequently used than the longer word(s) to which they refer. For example, individuals with no training or knowledge specific to the medical field may be familiar with the term CT scan, but few of those same individuals know that CT is an abbreviation that means “computed tomography.” (To begin memorizing these medical abbreviations and acronyms, as well as their corresponding meanings, refer to Tables 5–9 in Appendix A.)

 

 

Common Pharmacy Abbreviations and Acronyms

Name Exchange

In practice, you may see q.i.d. written as Q.I.D. or QID, while b.i.d. may also be written as B.I.D.  or BID. Learning the common abbreviations as well as the ways you can expect to see them written will help ensure that you are properly interpreting medication orders, CSP labels, and drug labels.

Abbreviations are also prevalent in pharmacy practice. They are typically seen on medication orders or other written communications between prescribers and pharmacies. Many of these abbreviations are derived from their Latin roots. For example, the abbreviation q.i.d. comes from the Latin words quater in die, meaning “four times a day”; the abbreviation PO is from the Latin words per os, meaning “by mouth.”

 

For sterile compounding personnel, pharmacy abbreviations provide important information, such as the name of a certain medication or the name of a specific intravenous (IV) base solution that is necessary for compounding a sterile preparation. For example, physicians or other prescribers often write orders for compounded sterile preparations (CSPs) using the abbreviations NTG (meaning “nitroglycerin”) and PCN (meaning “penicillin”). They may also include abbreviations when ordering IV base solutions, such as this physician’s medication order: 1,000 mL D5W w/KCl 20 mEq/L @ TKO. To understand this order, sterile compounding personnel would need to know what the abbreviations mean before beginning the compounding process. In this case, the physician wants the sterile compounding pharmacist or technician to compound a 1,000 mL bag of dextrose 5% in water with 20 milliequivalents per liter of potassium chloride, which would then be administered at an IV rate of approximately 10–40 milliliters per hour, which provides just enough fluid to keep the IV line open so that it may be used for blood draws and future treatment.

 

A medication order might also include abbreviations to communicate specific compounding directives. For example, a physician may order PF gentamicin sulfate for a neonate. Sterile compounding personnel would need to know that PF means “preservative-free” before preparing the medication for patient administration. Lack of familiarity with this abbreviation could lead to patient endangerment.

 

Pharmacy abbreviations are often used to indicate the route of administration and the dosing intervals for prescribed medications. For example, a medication order that contains the abbreviations IM and b.i.d. asks that a medication be administered intramuscularly, twice a day. Knowing these important abbreviations ensures the correct administration directives for medications. (To begin memorizing these common pharmacy abbreviations and acronyms, as well as their corresponding meanings, refer to Table 10 in Appendix A.)

 

 

Common Pharmacy Symbols

In addition to knowing pharmacy abbreviations, sterile compounding personnel need to recognize and understand a number of symbols specific to pharmacy practice. Symbols such as \( \uparrow \)­ and \( \downarrow \) are frequently used by prescribers on medication orders to indicate “increase” and “decrease,” respectively. For example, a physician or other prescriber may order a change in an IV flow rate to increase the dose of a medication, such as \( \uparrow \)­ heparin to 1,000 units/hr. Another common symbol is the Greek delta symbol (∆), which is used to indicate a desired change. For example, the medication order famotidine 20 mg ∆ from IV to PO communicates that the prescriber wants to change the route of administration from intravenous (IV) to oral (PO).

 

Because symbols are drawn by hand, and handwriting may be poor or at least vary significantly among healthcare personnel, symbols can easily be misinterpreted. Therefore, sterile compounding personnel should exercise care when using or interpreting pharmacy symbols. Typewritten or computer-generated symbols are preferred over handwritten symbols, whenever possible. (To help you recognize and interpret common pharmacy symbols, refer to Table 11 in Appendix A.)

Pharm Fact

TJC’s “Do Not Use” List of Abbreviations can be accessed online by going to the organization’s home page, entering “Do Not Use” in the search bar, and clicking on the “Do Not Use List of Abbreviations” on the menu. Healthcare personnel should be aware that new abbreviations are occasionally added to this list.

 

 

The Official “Do Not Use” List of Abbreviations

In 2004, The Joint Commission (TJC) created The Official “Do Not Use” List of Abbreviations in response to a number of sentinel events that had been noted across the United States. Due to the overwhelming evidence that certain frequently used abbreviations led to an increased incidence of medication errors, TJC integrated its “Do Not Use” List of Abbreviations into its standards for healthcare accreditation. The abbreviations on TJC’s list should not be used in any healthcare application due to their potential for misinterpretation and, consequently, patient harm. Healthcare personnel need to recognize nonstandard abbreviations used on a medication order and pursue additional clarification with the prescriber to ensure that a patient receives the correct medication or treatment.

 

 

List of Error-Prone Abbreviations, Symbols, and Dose Designations

Pharm Fact

The ISMP’s List of Error-Prone Abbreviations, Symbols, and Dose Designations and List of Confused Drug Names can be accessed online on the ISMP’s website. Personnel must register and then log in to access these free resources.

The Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) has also compiled a List of Error-Prone Abbreviations, Symbols, and Dose Designations to help healthcare personnel avoid dangerous documentation. This list, adopted by regulatory bodies and accreditation organizations, identifies abbreviations and other shorthand communication methods that have been associated with medication errors and, therefore, should not be used.

 

The ISMP also publishes a list of common look-alike and sound-alike drugs that often contribute to medication errors. This list, known as the ISMP’s List of Confused Drug Names, offers hundreds of drug names that are easily confused. The ISMP stresses awareness of such drugs, promotes adding a medical indication for each drug, and encourages electronic prescribing (e-prescribing) to avoid any ambiguity inherent to handwritten prescriptions.

 

 

Guidelines for Safe Electronic Communication of Medication Information

ISMP’s Guidelines for Safe Electronic Communication of Medication Information establish a uniform approach to transmitting medication information via an electronic format. The purpose of these guidelines is to prevent errors from inconsistent use of abbreviations, capitalization, and numbers. Specifically, the guidelines explain how to use decimal points, periods, and commas; when to use zeros; how to abbreviate dosage units; and what abbreviations to avoid.

 

In addition to these general communication guidelines, ISMP also provides recommended formats for certain notations, such as the following examples:

  • The name of a generic drug should be expressed using all lowercase letters; the name of a brand-name drug should be expressed with an initial capital letter, followed by all lowercase letters. The exception to this guideline is the use of tall-man lettering that provides a visual cue to healthcare personnel in the drug selection, preparation, and administration process. Tall-man lettering uses boldfaced, capital letters for emphasis of a specific part of a drug name.
  • Drug names ending with the letter “l” should be capitalized to avoid confusion created by the use of number “1” in the dose that follows the drug name. For example, the recommended format for the drug chloramphenicol would be as follows: chloramphenicoL 12.5 mg per kg of body weight every six hours.
  • Salt forms of drugs should only be included if there are multiple salts available or the salt alters the drug release, concentration, or dose.
