14.8 Understand the Resources and Supplies

USP <797> prescribes a number of requirements for the preparation of CSPs, including pediatric dilutions. This chapter and the corresponding lab provide resources and supplies that are in alignment with these directives.

 

 

Essential Supplies

USP <797>

Sterile compounding personnel must don sterile, powder-free, disposable gloves before preparing CSPs. With that in mind, all chapter references to “sterile gloves” indicate gloves with these characteristics.

Most sterile compounding procedures require the same essential supply items to be available for use in both an anteroom and a buffer room. For the anteroom, these supplies include foot and head garb that consists of low-lint shoe covers; low-lint head covers and (if needed) facial hair covers; face masks; and reusable goggles when eye protection is needed. Hand hygiene supplies include soap in a nonrefillable container; disposable nail cleaners; low-lint, disposable towels or wipers; an alcohol-based hand rub; and sterile 70% isopropyl alcohol (IPA). Other essential supplies include low-lint garments (gowns or coveralls) with sleeves that fit snugly around the wrists and that are enclosed at the neck and sterile, powder-free, disposable gloves.

 

Depending on a facility’s SOPs, sterile compounding personnel may perform the following procedures in the buffer room rather than in the anteroom: application of an alcohol-based hand rub prior to donning sterile gloves. Sterile compounding facilities must also have sterile 70% IPA available in the buffer room to spray sterile gloves prior to rubbing them together, or to saturate a wiper and then wipe sterile gloves. Sterile 70% IPA is used for procedure-specific purposes as well.

Procedure-Specific Supplies

In addition to the previously listed essential supplies, each sterile compounding procedure requires supply items specific to the procedure being performed. The type, number, and amount of procedure-specific supply items are determined by sterile compounding personnel prior to performing the procedure, based on information provided on the CSP label and the medication additive label.

 

After reading these labels, personnel perform one or more calculations that reveal the amount of additives needed to prepare the CSP. This information, in turn, dictates the number, size, and types of syringes needed to correctly withdraw the necessary ingredient volumes of the drug and diluent. Most sterile compounding procedures require the use of a Luer-lock syringe. Finally, sterile compounding personnel should obtain tamper-evident, sterile, adhesive seals if a tamper-evident, sterile syringe cap is not used. This type of seal is frequenly applied to an EEC’s vial stopper, or to a capped syringe, and is sometimes applied over the injection port of an IV bag, as a safety measure.

 

 

Pediatric Dilution Supplies

The process of compounding certain pediatric CSPs involves the manipulation of regular needles as well as medication, diluent, and EEC vials. Familiarity with the appearance of these items as well as any specific handling requirements is essential to sterile compounding procedures. (For specific information on the use of regular needles and vial techniques, see Chapter 10.)

 

 

Pediatric PF Gentamicin Vial

Name Exchange

Sterile compounding personnel should be aware that drug manufacturers may use the abbreviation PF (meaning “preservative-free”) either before or after the drug name on a medication label. Certain manufacturers may also include the term preservative-free (often in red type) on a medication label.

The drug used in this chapter’s procedural lab must be the pediatric-strength formulation of PF gentamicin. To verify this information, sterile compounding personnel should check the vial’s medication label for the word Pediatric (or Pedi), indicating that it is the pediatric-strength formulation of gentamicin, and for the words preservative-free or the abbreviation PF, indicating that it is a preservative-free medication. These designations often appear in red type on the label of a medication vial.

 

 

PF Diluent Vial

The type of diluent commonly used when preparing pediatric CSPs is normal saline (NS). When compounding sterile preparations for neonatal use, the diluent must be preservative-free (PF) as well (see Figure 14.3). Many pharmacies require the use of PF diluents for both neonatal and pediatric patients to avoid potential complications caused by the presence of a preservative such as benzyl alcohol. The type of PF NS diluent used for a pediatric CSP is generally supplied from the manufacturer in a single-dose container with a volume of either 10 mL or 30 mL.

 

 

EECs

Name Exchange

In practice, the terms single-dose and single-use are used interchangeably to describe a medication vial or container.

EECs are vacuum-sealed, sterile glass containers or empty polyvinyl chloride bags available in many sizes, including 2 mL, 5 mL, 10 mL, 20 mL, 30 mL, 50 mL, 100 mL, 150 mL, 250 mL, 500 mL, and 1,000 mL. As mentioned earlier, EECs are used to prepare custom-made CSPs using various components or ingredients that are not commercially available in the dosage, volume, or strength ordered by prescribers.

 

FIGURE 14.3 Medication Label for PF NS The diluent 0.9% sodium chloride, more commonly known as normal saline, is an isotonic solution used to maintain balance between the body’s vascular volume and interstitial spaces. © Paradigm Education Solutions

 

Occasionally, the base solution must be customized as well. For example, if a medication order calls for vancomycin 1 gram in 0.45 NS 250 mL IVPB, but the pharmacy only stocks the base solution 0.45 NS in a 1,000 mL bag, sterile compounding personnel must use secondary IV tubing to transfer 250 mL of fluid from the 1,000 mL bag of 0.45 NS into a 250 mL EEC. They must then inject the vancomycin into the custom-made 0.45 NS 250 mL IVPB base solution.

 

 

Syringe Caps

A syringe cap is a sterile, plastic cap used to cover either a slip-tip or Luer-lock syringe. Personnel must aseptically apply the cap to the tip of a filled syringe to avoid contamination of the CSP. Application of a sterile syringe cap protects the sterility of the CSP until patient administration.

 

 

Critical Sites of Essential Supplies and Pediatric CSP Supplies

Before beginning preparatory procedures in the anteroom or buffer room, sterile compounding personnel must recall the critical sites of the supplies (see Figure 14.4). Identifying the critical sites of each supply item helps personnel determine the proper procedure for handling the supply item once they have entered the buffer room and have begun working in a primary engineering control (PEC).

 

The critical sites of the pediatric dilution supplies include the needle, syringe tip, and the piston plunger and inner plunger shaft of the syringe. These critical sites are supplied by their manufacturers in a sterile form and should, therefore, not be swabbed with sterile 70% IPA prior to use.

 

Other critical sites of supply items used in the pediatric dilution procedural lab include the vial stoppers of the PF diluent vial and the PF gentamicin vial, as well as the vial stopper of the EEC vial. These critical sites must be swabbed with sterile 70% IPA prior to needle insertion to decrease the likelihood of contaminating the solution during injection or withdrawal procedures.

 

After identifying the critical sites of all supply items, personnel must exercise care when handling these items to avoid touch contamination, shadowing, or incorrect placement of the supplies within a PEC. (For additional assistance on the handling of critical sites of sterile compounding supplies, refer to Chapter 5 of this textbook.)

 

FIGURE 14.4 Vial and Needle-and-Syringe Unit The critical sites of a vial and needle-and-syringe unit must receive uninterrupted airflow from the high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filter of a PEC. For that reason, sterile compounding personnel must exercise care during the handling of these supply items to avoid touch contamination and shadowing. © Paradigm Education Solutions
