PART 1: CONCEPTS

16.9 Handling of Hazardous Drugs

Safety Alert

Sterile compounding personnel who are of reproductive capability must confirm, in writing, that they understand the risks of handling HDs.

USP <800> sets the standards for the handling of chemotherapy and other HDs. USP <800> applies to all healthcare personnel who receive, store, compound, dispense, transport, or administer HDs. It is also applicable to personnel who clean areas contaminated with trace amounts of HD residue and individuals who handle HD contaminated waste materials. To ensure safety for personnel who handle HDs, facility managers must establish policies and procedures that include the use of engineering controls (when required), proper PPE, and safe work practices. These policies and procedures must also reflect other national standards and applicable state and federal regulations.

 

 

Identification of HDs

USP <800> defines HDs as those drugs that appear on the NIOSH List of Antineoplastic and Other Hazardous Drugs in Healthcare Settings, a publication of the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH). Drugs that appear on the NIOSH List have displayed one or more of the following characteristics (in humans or animals): carcinogenicity, teratogenicity or other developmental toxicity, reproductive toxicity, organ toxicity at low doses, or genotoxicity. New drugs that have structure and toxicity profiles that mimic existing drugs determined to be hazardous also appear on the NIOSH List. In addition, the NIOSH criteria for HDs is used to evaluate investigational drugs and new drugs that become available after the most recent NIOSH List is released. If a facility determines there is a lack of information about an investigational drug or a new drug to classify it as hazardous, personnel should handle the drug as if it were deemed hazardous until more information is available.

 

Practice Tip

To view the current NIOSH List of Antineoplastic and Other Hazardous Drugs in Healthcare Settings, visit the following website: https://SterileComp2.ParadigmEducation.com/NIOSH.

The current NIOSH List, which was published in 2016, divides HDs into three groups:

  • Group 1: antineoplastic drugs (includes most chemotherapy drugs)
  • Group 2: non-antineoplastic drugs that meet one or more of the NIOSH criteria for a hazardous drug
  • Group 3: non-antineoplastic drugs that primarily have adverse reproductive effects (for both men and women who are actively trying to conceive and women who are pregnant or breastfeeding).

 

Facilities are required to create and maintain a list of HDs that are handled. This list must include any drug on the current NIOSH List.

OSHA and Hazardous Drugs and Chemicals

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) requires facilities to have a Hazard Communication Program for personnel before they begin their job responsibilities. This program must identify the hazardous chemicals or drugs in their assigned work areas, discuss the engineering controls in use, demonstrate PPE, describe safe work practices, and explain communication channels for the receipt of additional information or the inclusion of new hazardous chemicals or HDs. Personnel must be provided with specific information about these hazardous chemicals or HDs and must be trained in their proper labeling, transport, storage, and disposal. Part of this training includes how to read safety data sheets and, when applicable, chemical labels (see Figure 16.1).

Safety Data Sheets

A safety data sheet (SDS) must be maintained for each hazardous chemical or drug that is handled and must be readily accessible to personnel in their work areas (see Figure 16.2). Information for an SDS is based on the Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labeling of Chemicals, an international standard managed by the United Nations to replace various classification and labeling protocols used across the globe. An SDS offers a uniform template for detailed information on the physical and chemical properties of a hazardous chemical or drug, its associated hazards, safe handling practices for personnel, emergency control measures, transport information, and disposal considerations.

 

Name Exchange

OSHA safety data sheets are commonly referred to by their former name, material safety data sheets (MSDS), or simply the acronym MSDS.

 

FIGURE 16.2 SDS Form
OSHA requires chemical and drug manufacturers to provide an SDS for each hazardous product they manufacture. Courtesy of Spectrum Laboratory Products Inc., 2016

 

 

Hazardous Chemical and Drug Labels

OSHA requires that hazardous chemicals and drugs be labeled with warnings to alert users. Hazardous chemicals must be labeled with a pictogram and signal word that describes the hazard (refer to Figure 16.1). Hazardous drugs must be labeled with handling and/or disposal precautions (as applicable). To prevent mix-ups, some drug manufacturers utilize tall-man lettering, also known as mixed-case lettering, to draw attention to drug names that look or sound similar. For example, the chemotherapy drugs vinBLAStine and vinCRIStine are often printed in tall-man lettering on manufacturer-labeled vials, with the tall-man lettering emphasizing the differing parts of two similar words.

 

Tall-man lettering on the label of vinBLAStine and vinCRIStine provide a visual cue to individuals handling look-alike drugs. © Paradigm Education Solutions

 

 

Spill Management

A facility’s standard operating procedures (SOPs) must address the preventive measures and cleanup processes for HD spills. These procedures include the use of a spill kit if an HD spill occurs. A spill kit, commonly referred to as a chemo spill kit, contains supplies for containing and cleaning up a spill as well as appropriate signs for personnel to post that restrict access to the spill area. In addition to the cleanup process for HD spills, a facility’s SOPs must also designate the locations of these spill kits (receiving area of a facility and all other areas where HDs are handled), the capacity of these spill kits, and the PPE requirements for personnel who are cleaning up the spill.

 

If an HD spill occurs, trained and properly garbed personnel must contain the spill immediately, check the appropriate chemical or drug SDS for worker exposure limits and cleanup procedures, and then clean up the spill. All cleaning materials must be properly discarded into a hazardous waste container. Depending on the type of HD spill, personnel may also need to notify management and building services. Personnel who are exposed or potentially exposed to HDs must receive a medical evaluation immediately, and the circumstances and cleanup of the spill must be documented according to a facility’s SOPs.

 

A spill kit contains materials for cleaning up an HD spill as well as signs warning individuals to stay away from the affected area. George Brainard/© Paradigm Education Solutions

 

 

Receipt and Storage of HDs

USP <800> sets standards for the receipt and storage of chemotherapy and other HDs. These standards must be included in a facility’s SOPs and, consequently, must be followed by personnel who handle these drugs.

 

 

Receipt of HDs

Upon arrival in the receiving area of a facility, HDs should be encased in plastic. This plastic separates these drugs from other medications and helps to contain potential leaks. USP <800> requires that antineoplastic HDs that require manipulation (other than counting or repackaging) and all HD active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) be unpacked in a neutral or negative-pressure area and by personnel garbed in appropriate PPE, including chemotherapy gloves. These HDs must not be removed from their outer shipping container in positive-pressure environments or in the sterile compounding area.

 

Upon receiving HDs, personnel must inspect the packages for damage or signs of leakage. If they detect leakage, personnel must follow their facility’s SOPs and treat the leakage as an HD spill to prevent potential exposure.

 

 

Storage of HDs

Personnel must transport HDs to a designated HD storage area immediately after unpacking. USP <800> mandates that antineoplastic HDs that require manipulation (other than counting or repackaging) and all HD APIs be stored in an externally vented, negative-pressure room that has at least 12 ACPH. In addition, these HDs must be stored separately from other medications and in a manner that protects them from damage if they tip over and fall to the floor. HDs may not be stored directly on the floor.

 

 

Transport and Disposal of HDs

As discussed previously, NIOSH defines HDs that may be hazardous to healthcare personnel. However, the transport of HDs is regulated by the US Department of Transportation (DOT), and the disposal of HDs is regulated by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Healthcare personnel should be aware that DOT and EPA do not define HDs the same as NIOSH. For that reason, personnel may find that a drug with transport and/or disposal restrictions may or may not appear on the NIOSH List of Antineoplastic and Other Hazardous Drugs in Healthcare Settings. Therefore, it is important for individuals to understand the differences in HD requirements among these organizations.

 

 

Transport of HDs

HDs must be transported in accordance with applicable federal, state, and local regulations. Shipping vendors may have additional requirements, including packaging and labeling specifications. In addition, facilities must carefully select packaging containers and materials based on the mode of transport, so that they protect HDs from damage and leakage while maintaining the physical integrity, stability, and sterility of the drugs. Packages must also be labeled with the appropriate hazard warning and include storage and disposal instructions. Section 14 of the SDS lists federal requirements, but there may be additional state and local requirements.

 

Care must also be taken when HDs are transported within a facility to prevent contamination to personnel and the environment. Pneumatic tubes must never be used to transport liquid HDs or any form of antineoplastic HDs due to the possibility of breakage or leakage. HDs must also be labeled with any required handling precautions.

 

 

Disposal of HDs

HDs and HD-contaminated materials may require different disposal containers, depending on the type of waste. For that reason, it is critical that healthcare personnel are trained and comply with HD waste disposal regulations. Section 13 of the SDS lists federal requirements, but there are often additional state and local requirements.

 

Chemotherapy CSPs must be labeled with HD handling precautions for the protection of healthcare workers. iStock.com/fstop123

 

The Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) is a federal law that provides a framework for the EPA to manage the disposal of HDs and nonhazardous solid wastes. This law defines three categories of HDs: P-List, U-List, and D-List (characteristic hazardous waste). The drugs on these lists are often referred to as black box waste because they are discarded into a black hazardous waste container. These RCRA lists contain some drugs from the NIOSH List and some drugs that do not appear on the NIOSH List. Trace HD-contaminated drug containers and other materials are disposed of in yellow containers, typically associated with chemotherapy. Drugs that are not defined as hazardous to the environment are disposed of in medical waste containers. There is also a fourth waste container, found in a medical setting, that is used for blood-contaminated waste and other biohazards. Each type of waste container has specific handling and processing requirements that waste vendors must follow.
