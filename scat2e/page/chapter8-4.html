8.4 Understand the Resources and Supplies

USP <797> standards state requirements for hand hygiene and garbing. This chapter and the corresponding lab provide resources and supplies that are in alignment with these standards.

 

This section presents a specific order of hand hygiene and garbing procedures. However, this order depends on the placement of a facility’s hand-washing sink and on the facility’s SOPs. For that reason, sterile compounding personnel must refer to their facility’s SOPs for the hand hygiene and garbing process that must be followed by personnel.

 

 

Essential Supplies

Name Exchange

Disposable nail cleaners are commonly referred to as nail picks. This supply item should be stored in a manner that protects them from splash contamination that might occur in close proximity to the sink during hand washing procedures.

Most sterile compounding procedures require the same essential supply items to be available for use in both an anteroom and a buffer room. For the anteroom, these supplies include foot and head garb that consists of low-lint shoe covers; low-lint head covers and (if needed) facial hair covers; face masks; and reusable goggles when eye protection is needed. Hand hygiene supplies include soap in a nonrefillable container; disposable nail cleaners; low-lint, disposable towels or wipers; an alcohol-based hand rub; and sterile 70% IPA. Other garbing supplies include low-lint garments (gowns or coveralls) with sleeves that fit snugly around the wrists and that are enclosed at the neck and sterile, powder-free, disposable gloves.

 

Practice Tip

The specific garbing requirements, and location where garb is donned, depends on the Category of CSPs compounded (e.g., Category 1, 2, 3, HD). The order of garbing is determined by the facility and must be documented in facility SOPs. Refer to facility SOPs for specific guidance.

For the buffer room, supplies include low-lint wipers, agents required for cleaning and disinfecting the primary engineering control (PEC) and cleanroom suite, and waste containers. Sterile 70% IPA is needed to wipe sterile gloves and surfaces. A pen is needed for marking vials and may be needed for paper documentation. Depending on a facility’s SOPs, sterile compounding personnel may apply an alcohol-based hand rub and don sterile gloves in the buffer room, instead of the anteroom. If so, these essential supply items must be available for use in the buffer room. Facilities may also choose to use carts or tables to hold supply items.

 

 

Foot Garb

Personnel who work in a sterile compounding area must don appropriate footwear for safety and comfort. They should wear close-toed shoes because of the potential for injury by needles or broken glass. Clean, well-fitting walking or running shoes are a safe option and can provide personnel with the comfort and support they need for standing for hours in front of a PEC. Sterile compounding personnel also need to place low-lint shoe covers over their shoes to keep dirt and other substances from contaminating the floor of the sterile compounding area. These shoe covers are removed and discarded upon leaving the anteroom or segregated compounding area (SCA) per a facility’s SOPs.

 

 

Head Garb

Personnel in the sterile compounding area must also wear head covers, sometimes referred to as bouffant caps. These low-lint head covers must completely cover all of the hair, as well as the ears and forehead. Head covers are removed and discarded upon leaving the anteroom or SCA per a facility’s SOPs.

 

A bouffant-style head cover must completely cover the hair, ears, and forehead. © Joachim Wendler/Shutterstock.com

 

In addition, sterile compounding personnel must wear face masks to help catch the bacteria held in the liquid droplets and aerosols that emanate from the mouth and nose. Masks may have ties or elastic ear loops to secure them to the face, and most masks are made from paper or another nonwoven material and are discarded after each use. Personnel with facial hair must also wear facial hair covers, which are similar to face masks but cover all visible facial hair, including beards, moustaches, and sideburns. These facial hair covers are not a replacement for face masks.

 

A face mask helps prevent the bacteria in human secretions from contaminating the sterile compounding area. © Aleksandr Kurganov/Shutterstock.com

 

 

Hand Hygiene Agents

USP <797> does not dictate the type of hand cleaner or soap that sterile compounding personnel must use for handwashing. Rather, sterile compounding facilities are free to choose the agent that suits their operations and established procedures. Although regular, liquid soap is permissible, some facilities choose to use a hand cleaner with one of the following ingredients:

  • chloroxylenol
  • chlorhexidine gluconate
  • iodophors (such as povidone-iodine).

 

Hand sanitizing requires using an alcohol-based hand rub. © Shutterstock.com

 

At the conclusion of handwashing, sterile compounding personnel use low-lint, disposable towels or wipers to dry their hands.

 

Sterile compounding personnel must also perform hand sanitizing immediately prior to donning sterile gloves. For this process, personnel use an alcohol-based hand rub. After donning sterile gloves, personnel then apply sterile 70% IPA to all surfaces of the gloves and allow the IPA to dry thoroughly. The application of sterile 70% IPA occurs throughout the compounding process and whenever nonsterile surfaces are touched.

 

 

Scrub Uniforms

Best Practice

It is considered best practice for personnel to wear clean scrub uniforms, often referred to as medical uniforms, when preparing CSPs.

Personnel should don clean, noncotton, low-lint scrub uniforms (available in most hospitals or uniform supply stores) that should only be worn when preparing CSPs. Scrub uniforms are recommended because personnel may inadvertently contaminate the sterile compounding area with particles shed from their personal attire (street clothes). In addition, their personal attire may be damaged from cleaning and disinfecting agents or from exposure to drugs in the event of a spill.

 

 

Gowns or Coveralls

Sterile compounding personnel must wear, as the outer layer of clothing, either low-lint gowns or coveralls. As mentioned earlier, gowns or coveralls may be reused within the same shift, provided that they are not damaged or visibly contaminated. Finally, gowns and coveralls must remain in a classified area or within the perimeter of an SCA.

 

 

Gloves

USP <797>

Take caution when opening a package of sterile gloves as the exterior surface should not come into contact with anything other than the inside of the package. The interior surface of the gloves should only come into contact with your hands after you have completed handwashing, donned a low-lint gown, and have applied alcohol-based hand sanitizer to your hands.

Sterile compounding personnel must also don sterile, powder-free, disposable gloves. Synthetic, nonlatex gloves are available for people with latex allergies or sensitivities. As a general rule, personnel should note that size six gloves are small; size seven gloves are medium; and size eight gloves are large. Gloves should be close-fitting to allow for maximum finger dexterity but not so tight that they compromise an individual’s blood circulation or cause discomfort. Sterile compounding personnel should experiment until they find the glove size that fits best. Gloves are for one-time use only; personnel must discard them if they contain holes, punctures, or tears, and when leaving the sterile compounding area.

 

A pair of sterile, powder-free, disposable gloves is required for sterile compounding personnel. © Vach cameraman/Shutterstock.com

 

 

Procedure-Specific Supplies and Equipment

In addition to the previously listed essential supplies, each sterile compounding procedure requires supply items and equipment specific to the procedure being performed. For hand-washing procedures, a designated sink and hand-washing supplies must be available either inside or outside of an anteroom for use by sterile compounding personnel.

 

 

Hand-Washing Sink

Practice Tip

The terms handwashing and hand hygiene are sometimes used interchangeably. Hand hygiene may also refer to the process of sanitizing hands with alcohol-based hand rub.

USP <797> standards allow the sink used for hand hygiene to be placed either inside or outside of an anteroom and recommends that the sink should enable hands-free use. Beyond those standards, USP <797> provides no recommendations on specific sink features.

 

The hand-washing sink should be designated for use by sterile compounding personnel preparing to enter sterile compounding areas and should be clean and large enough and deep enough to minimize splashing during hand-washing procedures. It should also be free of any items, such as sponges or intravenous (IV) bags, that might cause splashing during these procedures. Finally, the hand-washing sink should have a gooseneck faucet with hot and cold running water and, preferably, electronic sensors or foot/knee pedals that allow hands-free operation of water flow. If a sink with hands-free operation is not available, personnel should let the water run throughout the entire hand-washing process and, upon completion of the process, use a low-lint wiper to turn off the faucet.

 

USP <797> states that an anteroom sink should enable hands-free operation. George Brainard/© Paradigm Education Solutions

 

 

Hand-Washing Supplies

Practice Tip

If facility SOPs do not call for the use of a surgical scrub sponge/brush, personnel must take extra care to ensure that all surfaces of the fingers, hands, and forearms are thoroughly washed by using a sufficient amount of hand cleanser or soap and water, applied with the fingers and hands. Take extra caution to wash around nail beds and in the creases of fingers, knuckles, and wrists.

SOPs regarding the use of surgical scrub sponges in the hand-washing process vary among sterile compounding facilities. Sterile compounding personnel must, at a minimum, perform the hand-washing process by first using a disposable nail cleaner to remove debris from underneath the fingernails while holding the hands under warm running water. They then proceed to wash their hands and entire forearms up to the elbows with soap and water for at least 30 seconds. Personnel should focus extra attention on areas that harbor micro­organisms, such as under the fingernails and in the creases of skin.

 

Anteroom sinks often have gooseneck faucets, pedals, and a deep basin to contain splashes. George Brainard/© Paradigm Education Solutions
USP <797>

Sterile compounding personnel should never use the brush side of a surgical scrub tool to clean the skin. Doing so may cause skin irritation or may cause skin particles to flake off into the sterile compounding environment, resulting in contamination.

 

Some facilities prefer their personnel to perform hand-washing procedures using a prepackaged, surgical scrub sponge/brush presaturated with an approved anti­microbial soap. The prepackaged, surgical scrub sponge/brush is a very effective tool for helping to ensure that handwashing has been performed efficiently. The disposable nail cleaner that is typically included in the package is used to clean under the fingernails and to remove contaminants that are invisible to the naked eye. The sponge side of the hand-washing tool is then used to clean the skin of the fingers, hands, and forearms up to the elbows. The brush side of the hand-washing tool is never used as it can cause skin irritation and excessive flaking of skin particles.

 

A nail cleaner and the sponge side of a surgical scrub sponge/brush are used by personnel during handwashing procedures. George Brainard/© Paradigm Education Solutions
USP <797>

Sterile compounding personnel must not use hand dryers because of the risk of creating air turbulence and circulating contaminants in the sterile compounding area.

 

At the completion of hand-washing procedures, sterile compounding personnel should rinse their hands and forearms so that soapy water runs off toward the elbows. This arm position helps to maintain the cleanliness of the fingertips, which will come in direct contact with the CSPs. They must then dry the fingers, hands, and forearms with low-lint, disposable towels or wipers. The receptacle in which the low-lint, disposable towels or wipers are stored should be designed so it does not compromise the cleanliness or integrity of these items.
