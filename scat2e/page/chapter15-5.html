PART 1: CONCEPTS

15.5 Prescribing Protocol for PN Therapy

Safety Alert

Prescribing guidelines for PN therapy generally provide typical dosage based on a patient’s body weight. However, ASPEN guidelines recommend that adult PN components and ingredients should be ordered per day. The PN order template and label should list each component’s amount per day. A prescriber should order the desired daily amounts based on a patient’s specific caloric and electrolyte needs.

PN solutions are often prescribed for hospitalized patients in critical care settings; however, they may also be prescribed for patients in ambulatory or home healthcare settings who need long-term nutritional support. Prescribing physicians collaborate with clinical pharmacists and registered dietitians to tailor nutritional formulas according to patients’ needs. These healthcare practitioners are often guided by the nutritional therapy standards published by ASPEN.

 

As mentioned earlier, ASPEN is a leader in the scientific study of best practices for safe prescribing, compounding, and administration of clinical nutrition therapy. ASPEN guidelines for PN therapy are published in the Journal of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition. Practitioners who prescribe and compounders who prepare PN solutions should follow these guidelines as well as USP <797> and relevant safety standards from the Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP). Prescribers, nutritionists, nurses, and pharmacy personnel involved in PN therapy should receive special training related to the particular complexities involved in the preparation and administration of this complicated type of compounded sterile preparation (CSP).

 

 

Indications for PN Therapy

Some medical conditions that may lead to the ordering of PN therapy include the following:

  • diseases of the stomach, bowel, or GI tract, such as Crohn’s disease, short- bowel syndrome, ischemic bowel disease, or ulcerative colitis
  • GI trauma or disease that results in gastrectomy or colectomy
  • congenital disorders of the GI tract
  • diseases such as cancer, stroke, or acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS)
  • severe cachexia, a type of malnutrition created by conditions such as marasmus or anorexia
  • any type of trauma, treatment, or disease that impairs a patient’s ability to receive nutrition by the oral or enteral route of administration (e.g., a GI tract condition or intensive, long-term radiation treatment to the throat region, which often inhibits a patient’s ability to swallow).

 

 

Guidelines for Prescribing PN Therapy

When ordering PN components, prescribers consider several factors, including the patient’s age, diagnosis, kidney function, liver function, nutrient and fluid requirements, and electrolyte levels. Table 15.1 provides a list of the typical PN base components and additives, as well as the ASPEN prescribing guidelines for an adult patient with a normal kidney function. These requirements are for a 24-hour period and are based on the patient’s body weight in kilograms, unless otherwise indicated in the table.

 

TABLE 15.1 Prescribing Guidelines for PN Base Components and Additives for an Adult*
PN Base Components Average 24-Hour Requirement for Patient with Normal Kidney Function
Sterile water for injection (fluid for hydration) 30–40 mL/kg/day
Dextrose (sugar) 5.8–7.2 g/kg/day
Amino acids (protein) 0.8–1.5 g/kg/day
IV fat emulsion (fat or lipid) 1 g/kg/day
PN Additives Average 24-Hour Requirement for Patient with Normal Kidney Function
Calcium 10–15 mEq/day
Magnesium 8–20 mEq/day
Phosphorus 20–40 mmol/day
Sodium 1–2 mEq/kg/day
Potassium 1–2 mEq/kg/day
Trace mineral elements 5 mL/day
Multivitamins 10 mL/day
*Based on Appropriate Dosing for Parenteral Nutrition: ASPEN Recommendations, which can be accessed at the following location: https://www.nutritioncare.org/uploadedFiles/Documents/Guidelines_and_Clinical_Resources/PN%20Dosing%201-Sheet-FINAL.pdf.

 

Medications other than the typical PN components and additives listed in Table 15.1 are usually not added to a PN solution due to the potential for incompatibility. However, there are four exceptions to this guideline:

  • insulin, which is occasionally added to help maintain a patient’s blood glucose level
  • an H2 antagonist such as ranitidine, which may be added to help prevent the formation of gastric ulcers
  • heparin, which may be added in a small quantity to help prevent clotting at the catheter site
  • cysteine, which is a type of amino acid that helps with glutathione synthesis.

 

Insulin, ranitidine, heparin, and cysteine have been determined to be compatible with most PN solutions. Trace minerals such as zinc, copper, manganese, or chromium may also be ordered individually or within a combined trace element solution, as necessary, per the discretion of a physician.

 

FIELD NOTES

During drug shortages involving PN components, sterile compounding personnel should follow ASPEN recommendations for drug rationing and conservation strategies. These recommendations, intended to be used only during drug shortages, are accessible at the following location: https://SterileComp2.paradigmeducation.com/PNcomponentshortage. Personnel should return to appropriate dosing as soon as the drug shortages have been resolved.

 

 

PN Medication Order

Safety Alert

To help prevent medication errors, ASPEN recommends that a CPOE system be used to prescribe PN therapy. Handwritten PN medication orders should be avoided when possible. If no CPOE system is available, a standardized PN medication order template should be used by prescribers.

Most facilities adhere to standard protocols and ASPEN guidelines for prescribing nutritional therapy for the following reasons:

  • the time, labor, and cost resources associated with PN preparation
  • the potential for serious medication errors or healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) associated with PN administration.

 

Like other types of medication, a licensed prescriber orders the components of a PN solution by completing a PN order form (see Figure 15.3). The form is then sent to the pharmacy for sterile compounding of the solution. In a facility that utilizes a computerized provider order entry (CPOE) system, a prescriber enters the PN order in a patient’s electronic health record. A facility that does not have CPOE should provide prescribers with preprinted, standardized PN order forms to ensure consistent and clear communication among healthcare personnel.

 

Whether a PN order is entered into the computer by a prescriber or is entered by a pharmacist or a pharmacy technician in the pharmacy, a corresponding PN label is printed in the pharmacy where pharmacy personnel check the label against the prescriber’s order to verify its accuracy and appropriateness. The CSP label, which may also be referred to as a PN label, should match the information on the PN order form exactly.

PN Flow Rate and Volume

Practice Tip

A PN bag typically contains an additional 100–250 mL to cover any lag time that may occur with tubing change and bag replacement This additional fluid ensures that the PN bag does not run dry in the interim.

Generally, a 24-hour supply of PN solution for an adult ranges in volume from 1,000–4,000 mL. A 24-hour supply of PN solution for a neonate ranges from 100–500 mL. Larger pediatric patients may receive daily PN volumes between 250–1,500 mL. The volume of solution prepared by sterile compounding personnel to provide a 24-hour supply to the patient is determined by the PN flow rate ordered by the physician. For example, if a prescriber orders a PN flow rate of 100 mL/hr, the pharmacy provides a 2,500-mL bag (the maximum hang time), and a new bag is hung at the same time each day.

 

There are some situations that may warrant a beyond-use date (BUD) that is less than 24 hours, such as when PN ingredients have a shorter stability period. In these instances, sterile compounding personnel should refer to USP <797> standards as well as their facility’s standard operating procedures (SOPs) and the information supplied by the manufacturers of PN ingredients to determine BUD. (For detailed information regarding BUDs for PN solutions, refer to Section 15.8 later in this chapter.)
