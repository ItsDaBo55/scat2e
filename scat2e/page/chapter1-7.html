PART 1: CONCEPTS

1.7 Compounded Sterile Preparations

Pharm Fact

Medications can also be administered by inhalation or by instillation into the eye, or they may be used to irrigate internal body cavities. Drugs administered via these routes are often provided by their manufacturers in a sterile form ready for patient administration but sometimes require preparation by sterile compounding personnel.

As mentioned earlier in this chapter, pharmacists and pharmacy technicians work collaboratively in a sterile compounding facility to prepare sterile products or compounded sterile preparations (CSPs). These sterile preparations are ordered by licensed prescribers for the treatment of illness or disease and the maintenance of optimal health in patients. The medication orders are then transmitted to a pharmacy via electronic prescribing or the use of a paper record.

 

CSPs are delivered to patients through the parenteral route of administration, or an entrance route to the body other than through the digestive tract. These sterile medications may be injected through various anatomical sites: a vein via the intravenous (IV) route of administration, a muscle via the intramuscular (IM) route of administration, the skin via the subcutaneous (subcut) route of administration or intradermal (ID) route of administration, the spinal column via the intrathecal (IT) route of administration or the bone marrow via the intraosseous (IO) route of administration. Because the IV route provides immediate access to the circulatory system, prescribers order this route when rapid, systemic drug delivery is needed. The parenteral route is also ordered when a patient’s condition warrants, such as an inability to swallow, a loss of consciousness, or a digestive system disorder. The majority of CSPs prepared by sterile compounding personnel are administered to patients through the IV route. Detailed information about CSPs and how they are prepared using aseptic technique is presented in Chapters 10–16.

Field Notes

As is the case with most pharmacy technicians, sterile compounding technicians tend to begin their careers in retail pharmacies. As technicians reach the top of the career ladder in that pharmacy environment, they are often drawn toward the institutional pharmacy setting or home health care where they may have more opportunities for career growth and the potential for higher pay. As a natural component of employment in these environments, pharmacy technicians are often cross-trained to work in the facility’s buffer room, also referred to as an IV room or a cleanroom.

 

Today, with the increased availability and quality of ASHP/ACPE-accredited pharmacy technician education and training programs, more pharmacy technicians are entering the institutional pharmacy setting directly out of school. These ASHP/ACPE-accredited programs provide high-quality education and training in general pharmacy policies and procedures, and many programs also provide excellent training in the more advanced area of sterile compounding and aseptic technique. For that reason, students who graduate from ASHP/ACPE-accredited programs have a better opportunity than others to move into the sterile compounding field, which will only grow as new drug treatments become available and patient-specific medication therapy becomes more common.

 

 

Types of CSPs

CSPs are classified as either large-volume or small-volume parenteral preparations. To prepare either type of solution, sterile compounding personnel add a medication or an additive to a base solution during sterile compounding procedures. The volumes of CSPs vary according to the route of administration (see Table 1.1).

 

TABLE 1.1 Typical Volume for Various Routes of Administration
intradermal (ID) = 0.1 mL
subcutaneous (subcut) = 1 mL
intramuscular (IM) = 3 mL

intrathecal (IT) 1–10 mL

(Larger volumes may be administered via a syringe pump or an epidural cassette pump.)

IV push (IVP) 1–60 mL

(Volumes = 10 mL are usually administered via a syringe pump.)

intravenous piggyback (IVPB) 25–250 mL

(Infusion rate is controlled via a tubing’s roll clamp or an IV pump.)

intravenous (IV) > 250 mL

(Infusion rate is controlled via an IV pump.)
The volumes given may vary according to the age or size of the patient and the dosing guidelines of the drug to be administered. Sterile compounding personnel should consult their facility’s SOPs manual, recognized pharmacy references, or the drug’s product insert for administration guidelines.

 

 

Large-Volume Parenteral Preparations

A large-volume parenteral (LVP) preparation is an IV solution that is typically greater than 250 mL that may contain medications, nutrients, or electrolytes. This type of parenteral preparation is compounded from a base solution such as 0.9% sodium chloride (more commonly known as normal saline or NS), dextrose 5% in water (more commonly known as D5W), or a Lactated Ringer’s solution (more commonly known as LR). Most hospitalized patients receive an LVP preparation during their stay. These IV solutions prevent or correct dehydration, restore electrolyte balance, and replace fluids in patients with significant blood loss. An IV access device provides easy vein access for blood draws and medication administration. LVPs are administered to patients as a continuous infusion or as an IV drip. (For more detailed information about LVPs, refer to Chapter 10.)

 

Normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) is available as both a small-volume parenteral solution (bag shown on the left) and a large-volume parenteral solution (bags shown in the middle and on the right). George Brainard/© Paradigm Education Solutions

 

 

Small-Volume Parenteral Preparations

A small-volume parenteral (SVP) preparation is a parenteral solution that has a relatively small volume ranging from 0.1 mL or less (for an ID injection) to 250 mL (for an intravenous piggyback [IVPB] solution). Sterile compounding personnel typically prepare SVPs that are delivered to patients through an IVPB or an intravenous push (IVP) method. SVPs are used for the intermittent IV administration of medication according to a prescribed dosing schedule. The majority of SVPs prepared by sterile compounding personnel are antibiotics. (For more detailed information about SVPs, refer to Chapter 11.)

 

 

Preparation of CSPs

Name Exchange

The anteroom may be referred to as the ante-area or transition room. The buffer room may be referred to as the cleanroom, sterile compounding room, or sterile prep room but is often referred to as the IV room.

As mentioned earlier in this chapter, USP <797> dictates the design and setup of the sterile compounding environment. Specifically, USP <797> standards include the establishment of a cleanroom suite consisting of an anteroom and a buffer room. An anteroom is a transition area that provides sterile compounding personnel with a space for gathering supplies, performing pharmacy calculations, donning garb, and performing hand hygiene procedures. A buffer room is an area in which sterile compounding takes place; this room is maintained as either a positive-pressure or negative-pressure environment through the use of a ventilation system that minimizes air contaminants. Specifically, sterile compounding procedures occur within a primary engineering control (PEC) in a buffer room. (To learn more about the sterile compounding environment, refer to Chapter 4.)

 

 

Use of Aseptic Technique

Maintaining aseptic technique in the preparation of CSPs is critical for the patient recipients of these sterile preparations. To minimize the risk of contaminants entering these sterile solutions, sterile compounding personnel must follow specific garbing and hand hygiene processes before entering the buffer room and must adhere to aseptic technique processes while performing sterile compounding procedures in a PEC. To ensure that these practices are consistently followed, personnel must undergo frequent and comprehensive evaluations based on tools such as competency assessment checklists and quality assurance and release testing procedures. These safeguards help ensure the safe preparation of CSPs. (For detailed information about these quality assurance measures, see Chapter 3.)
