15.12 Understand the Resources and Supplies

USP <797> standards prescribe a number of special requirements for the preparation of PN solutions. This chapter and the corresponding lab provide resources and supplies that are in alignment with the directives set forth in these standards.

 

 

Essential Supplies

Practice Tip

Sterile compounding personnel must don shoe cover, head cover, face mask, complete hand hygiene procedures, and don gown, then apply alcohol-based hand rub to hands, prior to donning sterile, powder-free gloves. Garbing supplies should be low lint. Single-use items should be disposed of after use; reusable garb must be relaundered and sterilized prior to reuse.

Most sterile compounding procedures require the same essential supply items to be available for use in both an anteroom and a buffer room. For the anteroom, these supplies include foot and head garb that consists of low-lint shoe covers; low-lint head covers and (if needed) facial hair covers; face masks; and reusable goggles when eye protection is needed. Hand hygiene supplies include soap in a nonrefillable container; disposable nail cleaners; low-lint, disposable towels or wipers; an alcohol-based hand rub; and sterile 70% isopropyl alcohol (IPA). Other essential supplies include low-lint garments (gowns or coveralls) with sleeves that fit snugly around the wrists and that are enclosed at the neck, and sterile, powder-free, disposable gloves.

 

Depending on a facility’s policy, sterile compounding personnel may perform the following procedures in the buffer room rather than in the anteroom: hand antisepsis using an alcohol-based hand rub and the donning of sterile gloves. Sterile compounding facilities must also have sterile 70% IPA available in the buffer room to wipe sterile gloves. Sterile 70% IPA is used for procedure-specific purposes as well.

 

 

Procedure-Specific Supplies

Name Exchange

Tamper-evident, sterile, adhesive seals are also known as tamper-evident IVA seals or, simply, IVA seals.

In addition to the previously listed essential supplies, each sterile compounding procedure requires supply items specific to the procedure being performed. The type, number, and amount of procedure-specific supply items are determined by sterile compounding personnel prior to performing the procedure, based on information provided on the PN order (and corresponding CSP label) and medication additive labels. After reading these labels, personnel perform one or more calculations that reveal the number and volume of additives needed to prepare the PN solution. This information, in turn, dictates the number, size, and types of syringes needed for the procedure. Most sterile compounding procedures require the use of a Luer-lock syringe a medication vial, an IV bag or other dispensing device, syringes, needles, and sterile 70% IPA swabs. If appropriate, sterile compounding personnel should obtain tamper-evident, sterile, adhesive seals. This type of seal is applied to the EEC’s rubber top.

 

The process of compounding a PN solution involves the manipulation of syringes, regular needles, and—in particular—vials and IV base solutions. Familiarity with the appearances of these items as well as any specific handling requirements is essential in sterile compounding procedures.

 

 

EEC

Practice Tip

EEC size can range from 2 mL glass vials up to 3 liter bags.

A 1,000 mL EEC is used as the final container into which the base components and additives are injected. An EEC contains a relatively negative air pressure, which helps to draw the fluid from the base component containers into the EEC. EECs larger than 100 mL often have a special type of aluminum cap that must be removed prior to use. Sterile compounding personnel must remove this cap carefully and in a specific order. Incorrect aluminum-cap removal from an EEC may result in a jagged aluminum edge, which is a hazard for an individual handling this supply item. The step-by-step procedure for proper aluminum-cap removal from an EEC is provided later in this chapter.

 

An EEC has an aluminum cap that must be removed carefully to avoid injury. © Paradigm Education Solutions

 

 

IV Tubing

During the PN compounding process, IV tubing enables the transfer of fluid from each of the source containers (base component bags) into the EEC. Before using the tubing, sterile compounding personnel remove and dispose of the tubing’s outer packaging. Then they aseptically remove the cap from the tubing’s needle adapter; attach a capped, regular needle to the adapter; and temporarily place the capped needle and attached IV tubing on the PEC’s work surface within the direct compounding area (DCA).

 

At one end of IV tubing is a needle adapter, or a site for the attachment of a regular needle. © Paradigm Education Solutions

 

Next, the tubing port plug is removed from the base component bag, and the cap is removed from the IV tubing spike. Personnel then insert the spike into the tubing port of the base component bag. (For reminders on the components and handling of certain supply items, refer to Chapter 5 of this textbook.)

 

Using aseptic technique, sterile compounding personnel then remove the needle cap from the other end of the IV tubing, remove the needle cap, and insert the needle into the top of the EEC. Once the desired volume of the first base component has been drawn into the EEC, personnel then move the roll clamp on the IV tubing to the closed position so that no additional fluid can enter the EEC.

 

The IV tubing spike is then removed from the first base component bag and aseptically inserted into the next base component bag. The required volume of the second base component is then run into the EEC, and the procedure is repeated with all subsequent base components.

 

 

PN Additive Preparation Supply Items

The additives needed for the preparation of PN solutions are provided in vials. (Refer to Chapter 10 of this textbook for standard vial handling techniques.) Sterile compounding personnel should select the appropriate-sized syringe to separately withdraw the correct volume of each additive from the vial. Once the PN base solution has been prepared, the additives have been drawn up, and both the base solution and additives have been checked by a pharmacist, personnel inject the additives in a designated order into the EEC.

Practice Tip

Some vented dispensing pins have a filter component (the plastic, disc-shaped structure located at the side of the item), whereas others do not. This component varies according to the manufacturer and to the procedure for which the vented dispensing pin is used.

 

In practice, the same additive vials may be used to prepare several PN solutions. To reduce the likelihood of coring, sterile compounding personnel may use a vented dispensing pin, which is a small, sterile, plastic spike with a Luer-lock syringe tip adapter at one end. Personnel insert the spike into the additive vial; then they twist the syringe onto the dispensing pin. Once the syringe is filled to the desired volume, personnel remove it from the dispensing pin and attach a capped needle. The procedure may be repeated, as necessary, to draw up multiple doses of the additive for numerous PN solutions, without inserting multiple needles into the vial. Consequently, coring is less likely to occur. (Note: Due to the high cost of providing separate dispensing pins for each additive and the fact that the procedural lab only requires one withdrawal from each additive vial, the Chapter 15 Procedural Lab requires the use of a capped, regular needle and syringe to draw up each additive.)

 

A vented dispensing pin may be used in PN solution preparation. © Paradigm Education Solutions

 

 

Critical Sites of PN Supplies

Safety Alert

Because manufacturers provide the syringe tip and all parts of the needle in sterile form, sterile compounding personnel must not swab the critical sites of these supply items with sterile 70% IPA. Doing so may result in contamination.

Before beginning preparatory procedures in the anteroom or buffer room, sterile compounding personnel must recall the critical sites of the supplies. Identifying the critical site of each supply item helps personnel to determine the proper procedure for handling the item once they have entered the buffer room and have begun to work in the PEC.

 

In addition to the critical sites of the syringe and needle, the critical sites of the procedure-specific supplies used in the Chapter 15 Procedural Lab include the IV tubing spike, the needle adapter, and the base component tubing port. These critical sites are provided in sterile form and, therefore, should never be swabbed, shadowed, or touched by anything other than the sterile device to which they are attached. Care must be taken to keep the critical sites within the DCA of the PEC so that these sites receive uninterrupted airflow from the high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filter.

 

Critical sites such as vial stoppers, base solution injection ports, and the rubber top of the EEC must be swabbed with sterile 70% IPA prior to needle insertion. Once these sites have been swabbed, personnel must exercise care to keep the critical sites within the DCA so that they receive uninterrupted airflow from the HEPA filter.
