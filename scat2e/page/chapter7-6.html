Part 2: Training

7.6 Examine the Resources and Supplies

Safety Alert

Sterile compounding personnel should refer to Appendix A to become more familiar with the medical terms, abbreviations, and symbols commonly used in pharmacy practice. Knowing this information allows personnel to accurately read and interpret medication orders, CSP labels, and drug labels.

A close examination of drug information on a medication order, a drug label, and a CSP label provides sterile compounding personnel with the data they need to perform pharmacy calculations and prepare a CSP that delivers the ordered medication dosage.

 

 

Identifying Drug Information on a Medication Order

Prior to performing the pharmacy calculations needed for the sterile compounding process, personnel must draw upon their knowledge of medication orders and labeling. Chapter 6 provides an in-depth introduction to medical terminology, abbreviations and acronyms, pharmacy symbols, and signa language used on a medication order. To accompany this background information, Appendix A offers a series of tables that list examples of the specialized pharmacy “language” seen on medication orders and labeling and in the communications among pharmacy personnel. Personnel should become familiar with the information in these tables in order to perform sterile compounding procedures safely and effectively.

 

Moreover, personnel must also be familiar with the generic and brand names of the drugs frequently used in sterile compounding (see Table 13 of Appendix A) as well as the common IV base solutions and IVPB fluids central to the compounding procedures (see Chapter 5). Memorizing these parenteral medications and solutions helps in the identification and understanding of these drugs on a medication order.

 

In addition to identifying common parenteral drugs and solutions, sterile compounding personnel need to be familiar with other components of a medication order, including dosage, dosing interval, and route of administration for each medication or ingredient on the order. Looking for words or abbreviations that identify a number, dose, volume, amount, or route can help in identifying the medications needed for sterile compounding (see Tables 7.5 and 7.6).

 

Best Practice

Many pharmacy computer programs automatically print the desired dosage volume of drug (i.e., the amount to draw up) on the CSP label. However, sterile compounding personnel should still verify the accuracy of the dosage volume by performing their own calculations. Personnel should use caution with the dosage volume information (sometimes called the mixing instructions) provided on the CSP label.

Medication orders are entered into an institution’s computer system, either through the use of computerized provider order entry (CPOE) technology or by the manual computer entry of a pharmacist or a pharmacy technician. Once the orders are verified by a pharmacist, a label is then printed for each CSP ordered. (Note: Due to the widespread adoption of CPOE technology by hospitals, medication order entry and label generation may occur in separate areas. For that reason, sterile compounding personnel may not have access to a print medication order. However, for the purposes of this lab, you will practice with the medication order and labels included in the lab portion of this chapter.)

For patient safety, it is important that sterile compounding personnel consider the context in which an abbreviation is used when determining the meaning of the abbreviation. For example, in some situations, the abbreviation IVF may refer to the medical procedure in vitro fertilization; in other situations, the abbreviation IVF may refer to intravenous fluid, such as dextrose 5% in Lactated Ringer’s.

Comparing the CSP Label to the Medication Order

To help ensure patient safety, it is considered best practice that the CSP label be compared to the medication order to verify that the label is 100% accurate. The sterile compounding technician or pharmacist who is tasked with preparing the CSP usually completes this comparison just prior to gathering the necessary sterile compounding supplies.

 

Best Practice

It is considered best practice to have both a pharmacist and a sterile compounding technician confirm that the CSP label matches the medication order.

To verify that the CSP label and the medication order match exactly, personnel should perform the verification process using the same method, in the same order, each time the verification process is performed. First, they should verify that the patient’s name (including the spelling) and room number (location) are identical on both the medication order and the CSP label. Next, personnel should confirm that the drug name and desired dose match. They must then confirm that the route of administration and either the dosing interval (for CSPs that are administered intermittently [such as IVPBs or IM injections]) or the infusion rate (for CSPs that are administered by continuous IV administration) are the same on both the medication order and the CSP label. Once all of the ingredients on the medication order have been matched and verified with the corresponding CSP labels, sterile compounding personnel should retrieve the ingredients and proceed to the next step: reading the information on the ingredient label.

 

 

Practice Tip

If a manufacturer’s ingredient label does not provide reconstitution information, sterile compounding personnel must consult the package insert provided by the manufacturer of the drug. They may also consult online references such as Micromedex or the Handbook on Injectable Drugs. In addition, personnel must consult their facility’s P&P manual to see if their facility uses predetermined reconstitution volumes that result in standardized concentrations for certain medications.

Reading the Ingredient Label

Manufacturers most often provide the medications or ingredients used in sterile compounding in either a vial or an ampule. Ingredients in these types of containers may be in a liquid or a powdered form. Parenteral ingredients in a liquid form do not need reconstitution, whereas parenteral ingredients in a powdered form must be reconstituted prior to use.

 

Regardless of the type or content of the ingredient’s container, each ingredient used in sterile compounding has a label that identifies the drug name, strength, or concentration, manufacturer’s name, expiration date, and lot number (refer back to Figure 7.1). In addition, powdered medications include instructions for reconstituting the powder. Sterile compounding personnel must carefully read the ingredient label information to determine the concentration (usually given in milligrams/milliliter [mg/mL]) of the drug in order to perform the necessary calculations.

Name Exchange

The words ingredient, drug, and medication may be used interchangeably especially as it refers to the manufacturer’s label.

Choosing a Calculation Method

After identification of the ingredients requiring sterile compounding and the verification of the accuracy of the CSP labels and concentrations, sterile compounding personnel must then choose a calculation method. The calculation method used depends on the information provided on the medication order, the CSP label, and the ingredient label. In most cases, personnel are required to determine the number of milliliters of injectable medication to draw up in order to provide the desired dose.

 

The criteria for each calculation method (outlined in Part 1 of this chapter) help to determine which method should be used to perform the calculation. As mentioned earlier in this chapter, possible methods for calculating sterile compounding problems include the basic formula, ratio and proportion, dimensional analysis, IV flow rate, IV drop factor, and alligation.

Performing the Calculation

Once sterile compounding personnel have determined the best calculation method, they write the equation on a piece of paper and verify that the correct information is in the correct place within the formula. Then they perform the calculation.
