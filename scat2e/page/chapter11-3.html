PART 1: CONCEPTS

11.3 Potential Complications of Parenteral Therapy

The administration of parenteral medications involves certain potential complications that may lead to injury or, possibly, death for patient recipients. Complications stem from the rapid, multisystem access of the circulating parenteral medications as well as the potential adverse reactions associated with their use. Therefore, all patients receiving parenteral therapy should be monitored for the following complications:

  • HAIs
  • allergic reactions (including anaphylaxis)
  • phlebitis
  • tissuing
  • embolism
  • extravasation
  • cellulitis
  • Stevens-Johnson syndrome
  • hepatotoxicity
  • nephrotoxicity.

 

Stevens-Johnson syndrome, a type of allergic reaction, starts as a diffuse red rash. If left untreated, the rash spreads deeper, causing the dermis to slough off. © Allen W. Mathies, MD/Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

 

For antibiotic SVPs, there is an increased likelihood for allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis; Stevens-Johnson syndrome; hepatotoxicity; and nephrotoxicity. Patients receiving these sterile preparations should be monitored closely for these complications.

 

 

IVPBs and Incompatibility Issues

In addition to these risks, IVPBs, due to their administration setup, have the potential for incompatibility issues with other IVPB or IVP medications. For example, if two incompatible medications are administered through the same IV tubing, a precipitate could form, clogging the IV tubing and potentially causing injury to a patient. For this reason, the primary IV tubing or the port (for patients without a primary IV line) should be flushed with a solution after the IVPB medication has been administered.

 

After administering hydration therapy to a hospitalized patient, nursing personnel often leave an IV line in place to administer an IVPB or take a blood sample. To perform these functions, a nurse removes the syringe and places a needleless injection port. Lisa McCartney/© Paradigm Education Solutions

 

A flush solution is typically dispensed by the pharmacy to the nursing unit in the form of a conventionally manufactured syringe or vial. If supplied as a vial, a nurse then withdraws the specified amount of solution just prior to flushing a patient’s IV line. At times, such as during a national shortage of conventionally manufactured flush solution syringes, sterile compounding personnel may be asked to prepare flush solution syringes from vials.

 

Practice Tip

Facilities may vary as to the type and volume of flush solution, as well as the frequency of injection port flush procedures. Sterile compounding personnel should consult their facility’s standard operating procedures (SOPs) to determine the procedures for port flushes implemented by the nursing staff.

To flush an IV line after administering an IVPB, nursing personnel typically lower the empty IVPB bag with attached secondary tubing to a height that allows gravity to force the primary IV solution to back up into the secondary tubing line. Once the primary IV solution has backed up into the IVPB, the IVPB is then tempo­rarily raised to a height above the primary IV solution. This action flushes any remaining medication out of the secondary IVPB tubing and into the patient’s vein. For patients without a primary IV solution, nursing personnel typically flush the port with a small volume (usually 3 mL, 5 mL, or 10 mL) of either NS solution or a heparinized saline solution.

 

 

Antibiotic Therapy and Superbugs

Sterile compounding personnel should be aware of a growing health concern in the use of antibiotic therapy: the development of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, or bacteria that are resistant to treatment by most of the commercially available antibiotics. These robust and virulent pathogens are known as superbugs and, consequently, are very difficult to treat.

 

 

Evolution of Superbugs

The evolution of superbugs has been influenced by three factors: the widespread use of antimicrobial agents, the overuse of antibiotics, and the prophylactic antibiotic treatment in the meat and dairy industries. All of these situations promote bacterial resistance to antibiotics due to repeated or extended exposure.

 

 

Antimicrobial Agent Overuse

In general terms, an antimicrobial agent is a substance or chemical compound capable of killing or inhibiting the growth of microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, protozoa, or viruses. Antimicrobial agents may be found in many hand soaps, cleansers, and surface disinfectants, as well as some medications. The rise in the use of these products has helped to strengthen various microbes, some of which are evolving to the point that standard antimicrobial agents may soon be in­effective against them.

 

 

Antibiotic Overuse

The prevalence of overprescribing antibiotics has also led to the development of antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Often, a prescriber yields to a patient’s demand for an antibiotic for an illness that may be viral, not bacterial, in nature. Because antibiotics are generally ineffective against viruses, the end result is simply an increased patient immunity to the prescribed antibiotic. So, when that patient is prescribed the same antibiotic for a bacterial infection, the drug is less effective against the strain of bacteria.

 

 

Prophylactic Antibiotic Treatment

Another source of antibiotic overuse comes from the use of antibiotics in the meat and dairy industries, whose policies allow for the administration of prophylactic antibiotic treatment to promote animal growth and to prevent and treat illness in livestock. Subsequent ingestion of the food products obtained from the livestock increases an individual’s exposure and may lead to development of resistance to certain antibiotics.

 

 

Development of Antibiotic Resistance

Bacteria develop antibiotic resistance in two ways. One method involves the mutation of genomes within the bacteria, allowing the micro­organisms to become immune to the effect of antibiotics. This type of antibiotic resistance is more common and is restricted to pathogens that share similar characteristics. The second method in which antibiotic resistance occurs is less common but more dangerous. Researchers have recently identified plasmids, or small, circular DNA molecules that provide antibiotic resistance to bacteria and other microbes. Plasmids make copies of themselves and can transfer their genetic material to other pathogens across a variety of classes of bacteria. This transfer allows microbes to develop resistance to multiple anti­microbials and antibiotics, thus increasing the potential of the evolution of microbes that are resistant to every drug known to humankind.

 

 

Superbugs as a Global Health Threat

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that more than 50% of antibiotics may be used inappropriately and that this inappropriate use increases the risk of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Superbugs such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (VRSA), vancomycin-resistant Clostridioides difficile (C. difficile), carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), and New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase (NDM-1) are on the rise and considered by many scientists to pose a serious threat to global health. Of particular concern is the incidence of resistant gram-negative bacteria such as Acinetobacter, E. coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae which often affect hospitalized patients. These superbugs are often resistant to multiple drugs, including very strong antimicrobial drugs such as vancomycin, methacillin, polymixin, and carbapenem. Since there are no stronger antimicrobials available, these drugs are often referred to as antibiotics of last resort. To address this concern, WHO launched a global campaign called the AWaRe tool to help reduce the incidence of antimicrobial resistance. According to WHO, the AWaRe tool “classifies antibiotics into three groups—Access, Watch and Reserve—and specifies which antibiotics to use for the most common and serious infections, which ones should be available at all times in the healthcare system, and those that must be used sparingly or preserved and used only as a last resort.”

 

Dr. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, WHO Director-General, warned about the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance in his statement below:

 

Antimicrobial resistance is one of the most urgent health risks of our time and threatens to undo a century of medical progress. All countries must strike a balance between ensuring access to life-saving antibiotics and slowing drug resistance by reserving the use of some antibiotics for the hardest-to-treat infections. I urge countries to adopt AWaRe, which is a valuable and practical tool for doing just that.

 

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) also monitors the growing threat from antibiotic-resistant bacteria in the United States. In 2019, the CDC published its “Antibiotic Resistance Threats in the United States” report that indicates that there are more than 3 million illnesses and 48,000 deaths in the United States annually from antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Armed with that data, the CDC built upon initiatives first identified in 2016, and published the “National Action Plan for Combating Antibiotic-Resistant Bacteria, 2020–2025.” This initiative will “support comprehensive and coordinated public health action against AR [antibiotic resistance] across all states and in several cities and counties.”
